 14.0 B How to use this chapter
  9.1   This chapter is written for adults, educators, community members, and service navigators. It explains
  9.1   forms of sexual violence and exploitation, what makes consent meaningful, how to respond safely, and
  9.1   where to seek support. Readers may skip examples or activities if they feel uncomfortable. No one
  9.1   needs to share personal experiences to complete the chapter.
  9.1   Learning outcomes: After reading, a learner should be able to recognize coercion and abuse of
  9.1   authority; explain sexual harassment and exploitation; identify safer first responses; understand
  9.1   time-sensitive healthcare; and describe Rwanda-based support options.
 14.0 B 1. Understanding sexual violence
  9.1   Sexual violence refers to sexual acts, attempted sexual acts, or other sexual conduct carried out without
  9.1   valid consent, including through force, threats, intimidation, coercion, exploitation of vulnerability, or
  9.1   abuse of power. It can happen between strangers, acquaintances, relatives, colleagues, partners, or
  9.1   spouses. It may occur in person or through technology. Sexual violence is never justified by a person’s
  9.1   clothing, relationship history, social status, or previous consent. [1, 2]
  9.1   Sexual violence is not limited to physically violent assault. It may involve unwanted sexual touching,
  9.1   forced or coerced sexual activity, attempted sexual assault, sexual exploitation, or non-consensual
  9.1   creation or sharing of sexual images. The exact legal definitions and offences differ by jurisdiction and
  9.1   must be checked against Rwandan law when legal information is published.

 14.0 B 2. Consent: what makes agreement meaningful?
  9.1   Consent is a freely given, informed, specific and ongoing agreement to a particular activity. Someone
  9.1   may change their mind. Being married, dating, accepting a gift, going to someone’s room, or agreeing
  9.1   to one activity does not imply agreement to another. Silence, freezing, or absence of physical resistance
  9.1   is not by itself consent. [1, 2]
 10.5 B When agreement is not freely given
  9.0   • A threat is made: for example, a supervisor suggests a student will fail an assessment unless they
  9.0   accept a sexual demand.
  9.0   • Someone is unable to make an informed decision, for example due to unconsciousness or
  9.0   incapacitation.
  9.0   • A person agrees because they fear injury, humiliation, losing a job, housing, essential support, or
  9.0   another serious consequence.
  9.0   • A power imbalance is exploited: an authority figure controls grades, employment, aid, healthcare, or
  9.0   access to services.
  9.1   The age and capacity of a child require separate safeguarding and legal rules. A child’s apparent
  9.1   agreement does not remove the need for child protection and legal assessment.
 14.0 B 3. Sexual harassment
  9.1   Sexual harassment includes unwelcome sexual comments, requests, messages, gestures, images,
  9.1   touching, or other sexual conduct that violates dignity or creates an intimidating, hostile, humiliating, or
  9.1   offensive environment. It can occur in schools, workplaces, public transport, markets, health facilities,
  9.1   community organizations, or online spaces.
 10.5 B Two recurring patterns
  9.1   Pressure linked to rewards or penalties: A person with authority requests sexual contact in exchange for
  9.1   a grade, placement, promotion, contract, or service. Hostile environment: Repeated sexual jokes,
  9.1   unwanted advances or degrading conduct make studying or working unsafe. A single serious incident
  9.1   can also warrant attention.
  9.1   Examples: A lecturer repeatedly sends sexual messages to a student after being asked to stop; a
  9.1   manager threatens to reduce shifts following a rejected advance; commuters make unwanted sexual
  9.1   contact on a bus. The person experiencing harassment is not responsible for managing the offender’s
  9.1   behavior.
 14.0 B 4. Sexual exploitation and abuse of authority
  9.1   Sexual exploitation involves abusing a position of vulnerability, differential power, or trust for sexual
  9.1   purposes, including situations in which a person seeks sexual access or advantage by controlling
  9.1   something the other person needs. A person may appear to agree while facing severe inequality in
  9.1   available choices. [3]

  9.1   An aid worker who conditions assistance on sexual acts, a recruiter who demands sexual favors for
  9.1   employment, or a caretaker who uses dependence to pressure someone are illustrative forms of
  9.1   exploitation. Such conduct may also breach safeguarding obligations, professional ethics, and criminal
  9.1   law.
 10.5 B Why power matters
  9.1   Power can come from grades, money, employment, legal or immigration status, access to food and
  9.1   shelter, professional authority, social influence, physical strength, or control over confidential
  9.1   information. Ethical organizations must define prohibitions, reporting routes, retaliation protections, and
  9.1   independent responses instead of leaving an affected person to negotiate with the person holding
  9.1   power.
 14.0 B 5. Sexual coercion and intimidation
  9.1   Coercion is pressure that undermines a person’s freedom to choose. It can include threats of violence,
  9.1   blackmail, threats to disclose intimate images, persistent pressure after refusal, threats to withdraw
  9.1   financial support, manipulation of dependency, or abuse of institutional authority. Coercion can occur
  9.1   without visible injury.
  9.1   A healthy boundary is respected without argument, retaliation, or punishment. Repeated requests after
  9.1   a refusal, monitoring a person’s movements, or threatening their reputation should be treated as
  9.1   concerning behavior rather than romantic persistence.
 14.0 B 6. Technology-facilitated sexual harm
  9.1   Sexual harassment and coercion may occur through messaging, social networks, devices, and digital
  9.1   services. Examples include unsolicited sexual messages, demands for intimate photos, threats to
  9.1   publish images, sharing private sexual images without consent, impersonation, location-based stalking,
  9.1   or harassment across multiple accounts.
  9.1   A person can consent to taking a photograph without consenting to its publication. Consent to share one
  9.1   message or image does not authorize redistribution. People experiencing digital abuse may need a
  9.1   tailored safety plan, especially if an offender can access their device or accounts.
 10.5 B Safer first considerations
  9.0   • If safe, use a device or account the offender cannot access when seeking support.
  9.0   • Avoid confronting the person or changing settings abruptly if those actions could increase danger.
  9.0   • If safe and desired, preserve relevant messages or links without redistributing intimate content. For
  9.0   content involving children, seek qualified reporting guidance and do not save or share illegal imagery.
  9.0   • Ask a trusted specialist or relevant authority about privacy, reporting, and evidence options.

 14.0 B 7. Effects on wellbeing and daily life
  9.1   Responses to sexual violence differ. Some survivors feel fear, anger, shame, sadness, numbness, or
  9.1   confusion; others may initially show little visible distress. Possible effects include injury, sexually
  9.1   transmitted infections, unintended pregnancy, sleep problems, traumatic stress, difficulty
  9.1   concentrating, withdrawal, and disruptions to education, work, and relationships. These are possible
  9.1   outcomes, not inevitable outcomes or criteria for believing someone.
  9.1   People may delay disclosing because of fear, dependence, stigma, threats, family pressure, lack of safe
  9.1   services, or uncertainty about confidentiality. A delayed disclosure or fragmented memory does not
  9.1   prove an account false. Support should not depend on whether someone reports to police.
 14.0 B 8. Responding to a disclosure: first-line support
  9.1   The WHO clinical handbook emphasizes attention to immediate emotional needs, physical needs,
  9.1   ongoing safety, and longer-term support. The LIVES approach summarizes listening, inquiring about
  9.1   needs and concerns, validating, enhancing safety, and supporting access to services. These are
  9.1   principles for trained responders and should not be confused with conducting an investigation. [1]
 10.5 B Supportive words and actions
  9.0   • Listen without interruption, interrogation, disbelief, or blame. A person is entitled to pause or stop.
  9.0   • Acknowledge the experience: “Thank you for telling me. You deserve to be safe.”
  9.0   • Ask about immediate needs: “Are you safe right now? Would you like help finding medical care or
  9.0   another support service?”
  9.0   • Explain any limits to confidentiality before collecting sensitive details, especially when a child is
  9.0   involved.
  9.0   • Offer choices. Do not pressure someone to reconcile, confront the offender, make a public disclosure,
  9.0   or take a specific legal step.
 10.5 B Avoid these responses
  9.1   Do not ask why the survivor was in a location, why they did not fight, or why they waited to disclose. Do
  9.1   not request a repeated account merely to satisfy curiosity, offer guarantees about outcomes, or promise
  9.1   absolute secrecy. Do not mediate between survivor and alleged offender as a substitute for protection
  9.1   and professional response.
 14.0 B 9. Urgent healthcare after sexual assault
  9.1   Time matters: If sexual assault was recent, seek medical care as soon as safely possible, ideally
  9.1   immediately. HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP), when clinically indicated, should start as soon as
  9.1   possible and no later than 72 hours after a potential exposure. Emergency contraception may also
  9.1   be time-sensitive; a clinician should advise on available methods and their effective windows. Even
  9.1   after these windows, medical care and support remain important. [4, 5]

  9.1   Healthcare assessment may include treatment of injuries, assessment for HIV PEP, prevention or testing
  9.1   for sexually transmitted infections, emergency contraception where appropriate, other reproductive
  9.1   healthcare, psychological first-line support, and discussion of forensic documentation. The survivor
  9.1   should receive information and be asked for consent to examinations and procedures, subject to
  9.1   applicable safeguarding and legal requirements. [1, 4]
  9.1   Do not delay medical care because you are unsure whether to report to police, or because clothing was
  9.1   changed or washing has occurred. Evidence options can be discussed by trained local providers. If
  9.1   someone is bleeding heavily, has severe injuries, is unconscious, or faces imminent danger, emergency
  9.1   care is the priority.
 14.0 B 10. Safeguarding children and other vulnerable people
  9.1   Children require an age-appropriate, trained response that prioritizes protection, avoids suggestive
  9.1   questioning, and follows applicable mandatory reporting, consent, and guardian rules. An accompanying
  9.1   adult is not always a safe guardian. Avoid asking the child to repeat a detailed account; record only
  9.1   essential facts according to authorized procedures and refer promptly to designated protection
  9.1   professionals.
  9.1   People with disabilities, displaced persons, people dependent on caregivers, and others facing barriers
  9.1   may need accessible communication, interpreters, safe transport, or independent advocacy. Never
  9.1   assume that a survivor cannot make decisions merely because they need support.
 14.0 B 11. Rwanda: care and referral pathways
  9.1   Rwanda’s Isange One Stop Centres provide integrated services for GBV survivors, bringing together
  9.1   healthcare, psychosocial support, investigation and legal pathways. Government sources describe
  9.1   services as free and available through the national network. [6, 7]
 10.5 B Published contacts (verify before public deployment)
  9.0   • Isange One Stop Centre: 3029 (listed by RIB and UNHCR Rwanda).
  9.0   • Rwanda Investigation Bureau: 3512 for domestic/GBV reporting; 116 for child abuse; 166 for
  9.0   emergencies or crime information (RIB contact page).
  9.0   • Other GBV referral information: MIGEPROF lists 9059 and Gender Monitoring Office 5798.
  9.1   Important: UNHCR Rwanda notes that approaching an Isange Centre may lead to a report to law
  9.1   enforcement. Service users should ask how information will be shared and what confidentiality or
  9.1   child-protection obligations apply. Do not promise that a particular referral is anonymous or will avoid
  9.1   police involvement. [6, 7, 8]
 14.0 B 12. Prevention in schools, workplaces, and institutions
  9.1   Preventing sexual harassment and exploitation requires clear rules and accountable systems, not only
  9.1   individual awareness. Educational institutions and employers should prohibit sexual demands linked to
  9.1   grades, placement, aid, recruitment, or promotion; train staff; provide confidential and accessible
  9.1   reporting channels; protect complainants and witnesses from retaliation; and arrange independent,
  9.1   timely, fair responses.

  9.1   A safe learning environment includes respectful boundaries, transparent assessment procedures,
  9.1   safeguarding responsibilities, and accessible support for students. A workplace should establish clear
  9.1   behavioral expectations and make reporting possible without forcing disclosure to the alleged offender’s
  9.1   supervisor.
 14.0 B 13. Fictional learning scenarios
 10.5 B Scenario A: University placement
  9.1   A supervisor privately suggests an internship evaluation will improve if a student agrees to a sexual
  9.1   relationship. The student feels unable to refuse because graduation depends on the placement.
  9.1   Analysis: This is an abuse-of-authority concern. A trustworthy institutional reporting channel, protection
  9.1   from retaliation, and independent support are important; the student should not be expected to
  9.1   negotiate with the supervisor.
 10.5 B Scenario B: Workplace messages
  9.1   A colleague sends repeated sexual messages after being asked to stop. A team leader dismisses the
  9.1   situation as a joke. Analysis: Unwelcome sexual communication can constitute harassment. Institutional
  9.1   responsibility includes safe reporting and proportionate responses.
 10.5 B Scenario C: Digital blackmail
  9.1   A former partner threatens to send an intimate image to a family group unless the person agrees to
  9.1   meet. Analysis: Threats to distribute images are coercive and can heighten risk. The person may need
  9.1   digital safety support and confidential professional advice rather than being told to confront the former
  9.1   partner.
 10.5 B Scenario D: Recent assault
  9.1   An adult tells a trusted friend that they were sexually assaulted the previous evening but do not want to
  9.1   report the incident yet. Analysis: The friend can explain that medical assessment is time-sensitive, offer
  9.1   to help reach an Isange Centre or hospital, and avoid pressuring the person into disclosure.
  9.1   Reporting-related confidentiality limits should be explained accurately by providers.
 10.5 B Scenario E: Child safeguarding
  9.1   A pupil says an adult at school has repeatedly requested sexual contact and threatened punishment if
  9.1   they tell anyone. Analysis: The priority is immediate protection and referral to trained child-protection
  9.1   responders. Adults should avoid investigating independently or promising secrecy.
 14.0 B 14. Guided educational activities
 10.5 B Activity 1: Recognize the power imbalance
  9.1   In pairs or individually, identify who controls a resource in Scenario A, what consequence is being
  9.1   threatened, and why an apparent “yes” may not represent freely given consent. Suggested response:
  9.1   The supervisor controls evaluation; the student risks an educational penalty; this undermines free
  9.1   choice.

 10.5 B Activity 2: Practice supportive language
  9.1   Rewrite “Why did you go there?” as a survivor-centred question. Suggested response: “What would help
  9.1   you feel safer right now?” Do not ask the learner to reveal personal experiences.
 10.5 B Activity 3: Choose a safer digital response
  9.1   For Scenario C, describe two considerations before changing device settings or messaging the
  9.1   threatening person. Suggested response: Evaluate whether the person monitors the device, and identify
  9.1   a safer device or professional support contact.
 10.5 B Activity 4: Sort urgent and non-urgent needs
  9.1   Classify the following: severe injury; a need for HIV exposure assessment after yesterday’s assault; a
  9.1   request for information about school policy. Suggested response: Injury and recent exposure warrant
  9.1   urgent healthcare; the policy question needs a safe institutional information channel.
 10.5 B Activity 5: Map a referral without assumptions
  9.1   Using Rwanda’s publicly listed contacts, outline a referral that offers choice and explains possible
  9.1   information-sharing. Suggested response: Ask whether the person is safe and wants medical/support
  9.1   options, share the Isange contact 3029, explain that providers can clarify reporting obligations, and
  9.1   support safe access without making confidentiality promises.
 14.0 B 15. Knowledge assessment with answer explanations
 10.5 B 1. Does a previous agreement imply consent to future sexual activity?
  9.1   Answer: No. Consent is specific and ongoing.
 10.5 B 2. Can a threat to fail a student undermine consent?
  9.1   Answer: Yes. It exploits unequal power and threatens a serious consequence.
 10.5 B 3. Is sexual harassment limited to physical contact?
  9.1   Answer: No. Unwelcome sexual messages, comments and other conduct can constitute harassment.
 10.5 B 4. Can sharing an intimate image without permission cause sexual harm?
  9.1   Answer: Yes. Image-based abuse violates privacy and can involve coercion.
 10.5 B 5. Should a friend demand a detailed account from a survivor?
  9.1   Answer: No. Listen, validate, and offer options without interrogation.
 10.5 B 6. What is the critical HIV PEP window?
  9.1   Answer: As soon as possible, ideally within 24 hours and no later than 72 hours after potential exposure,
  9.1   based on clinical assessment.

 10.5 B 7. Does seeking help always guarantee no police reporting?
  9.1   Answer: No. Applicable law and service processes may create reporting or information-sharing
  9.1   obligations.
 10.5 B 8. Is medical care worthwhile after 72 hours?
  9.1   Answer: Yes. Injury care, STI assessment, reproductive care and emotional support may still be needed.
 10.5 B 9. Should teachers privately investigate suspected child sexual abuse?
  9.1   Answer: No. Follow approved safeguarding pathways and avoid repeated questioning.
 10.5 B 10. What should institutions provide?
  9.1   Answer: Clear prevention policy, accessible reporting, retaliation protections and independent action.
 14.0 B 16. Key terms
  9.1   Consent: Freely given, informed and specific agreement that can be withdrawn.
  9.1   Sexual coercion: Pressure, threats or exploitation that undermines a free choice.
  9.1   Sexual harassment: Unwelcome sexual conduct that violates dignity or creates an intimidating or
  9.1   offensive environment.
  9.1   Sexual exploitation: Abuse of vulnerability, power or trust for sexual purposes.
  9.1   Safeguarding: Policies and actions intended to prevent and respond to harm, especially involving
  9.1   children and vulnerable people.
  9.1   PEP: Time-sensitive antiretroviral medication used after a possible HIV exposure when clinically
  9.1   indicated.
  9.1   Survivor-centred support: Support grounded in dignity, safety, privacy, informed choice and appropriate
  9.1   services.
  9.1   Forensic documentation: Professional medical and evidentiary records collected under applicable
  9.1   procedures and consent requirements.
