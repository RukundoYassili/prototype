 14.5 B How to use this chapter
  9.3   This chapter is designed for readers, educators, community facilitators, and GBV awareness
  9.3   programs. Readers may skip activities or pause at any time. Do not ask people to disclose personal
  9.3   experiences during group learning.
  9.5   Safety first: Avoid instructions that could increase danger, such as changing a monitored PIN,
  9.5   confronting an abusive person, or secretly moving funds without individualized safety advice.
 14.5 B Learning objectives
  9.2   • Define economic abuse and distinguish it from ordinary financial disagreements.
  9.2   • Recognize economic control, exploitation, employment sabotage, forced debt, and restrictions on
  9.3   essential needs.
  9.2   • Understand why economic dependence may limit choices without blaming survivors.
  9.2   • Identify safer, survivor-led support and appropriate referrals in Rwanda.
  9.2   • Apply the concepts to fictional examples and check understanding.
 14.5 B 1. Understanding economic violence
  9.3   Economic violence or economic abuse occurs when a person uses money, employment, property,
  9.3   debt,
  9.3   education, benefits, or access to resources to control, exploit, or harm another person. Financial
  9.3   abuse
  9.3   is often used for direct control of income, accounts, assets, and spending; economic abuse is wider
  9.3   and
  9.3   can also undermine a person's ability to obtain or keep resources. It can occur in intimate
  9.3   relationships,
  9.3   families, care arrangements, and other settings. [1, 2]
  9.3   The central concern is not whether partners share an account or earn different amounts. It is
  9.3   whether
  9.3   someone uses threats, deception, intimidation, coercion, or unequal power to deny another person
  9.3   genuine choice and access to necessary resources. A single incident can be serious, while some
  9.3   abuse
  9.3   develops as a repeated pattern.

  9.5   Key principle: Having less income, depending on family members, or choosing to share
  9.5   finances
  9.3   does not itself mean abuse. Control, coercion, exploitation, and the person's freedom to make
  9.3   decisions are critical to understanding the situation.
 14.5 B 2. Why economic abuse matters
  9.3   Economic abuse can make it harder to afford food, medication, transport, safe housing, school
  9.3   fees, or
  9.3   communication. It can undermine financial independence, interrupt education or employment,
  9.3   create
  9.3   debt, and make asking for help or leaving an abusive situation more difficult. These barriers can
  9.3   continue after a relationship ends. [2, 3]
  9.3   Economic abuse may occur together with emotional, physical, sexual, or technology-facilitated
  9.3   abuse.
  9.3   The presence of economic abuse does not establish that all other types are present, but it should
  9.3   prompt careful attention to the person's safety and choices.
 14.5 B 3. Forms of economic violence
 10.6 B 3.1 Restricting access to money and essentials
  9.3   A controlling person may keep all income, refuse access to funds for necessary food or treatment,
  9.3   require receipts for every small purchase as a form of surveillance, confiscate earnings, or deny
  9.3   money
  9.3   for transport to seek help. Restrictions can become especially harmful when a person has children,
  9.3   a
  9.3   disability, or health-related needs.
 10.6 B 3.2 Economic exploitation and property abuse
  9.3   This can include taking savings, livestock, land-related proceeds, inventory, or personal
  9.3   possessions
  9.3   without agreement; using another person's bank or mobile-money account without permission;
  9.3   pressuring someone to transfer property; or diverting income from a jointly run business. The exact
  9.3   legal classification depends on the circumstances and applicable Rwandan law.
 10.6 B 3.3 Employment and education sabotage
  9.3   An abusive person may forbid employment or schooling, repeatedly interrupt work, damage tools,

  9.3   interfere with a job interview, refuse reasonable childcare arrangements as a tactic of control, or
  9.3   harass
  9.3   someone at their workplace until they lose opportunities. Sabotage can make long-term
  9.3   independence
  9.3   harder.
 10.6 B 3.4 Forced debts, loans, and financial documents
  9.3   Someone may be pressured to borrow money, become a guarantor, hand over a PIN, sign a
  9.3   contract
  9.3   they do not understand, or accept liability for another person's purchases. A debt may become a
  9.3   mechanism of control when consent is obtained through threats or deception. A person should be
  9.3   able
  9.3   to seek an independent explanation before making a financial commitment.
 10.6 B 3.5 Control through technology
  9.3   Digital economic abuse may involve monitoring mobile-money messages, demanding wallet or
  9.3   banking
  9.3   PINs, accessing online accounts, requiring location-sharing to authorize purchases, or using shared
  9.3   devices to track transactions. Changing access on a monitored device can sometimes alert an
  9.3   abusive
  9.3   person, so digital safety should be considered before acting.
 10.6 B 3.6 Withholding support and exploiting caregiving
  9.3   A family member or caregiver might control an older person's funds, disability-related resources,
  9.3   pension, or essential belongings. Economic abuse can affect people of any gender and age. Child
  9.3   safeguarding and other legal duties may apply when a child or dependent person is involved.
 14.5 B 4. Financial partnership versus financial control
  9.3   Healthy collaboration: Partners may voluntarily choose a shared budget, discuss priorities, give one
  9.3   person responsibility for paying bills, or agree to save for a business. Each person can ask
  9.3   questions,
  9.3   understand important decisions, raise concerns without fear, and seek access to necessities.
  9.3   Concerning control: One partner threatens punishment for asking about finances, takes earnings
  9.3   without permission, insists on secrecy, prevents work, or uses access to basic necessities to force
  9.3   compliance. What matters is the power dynamic and freedom to disagree, not simply the
  9.3   household
  9.3   accounting arrangement. [1, 3]

 14.5 B 5. Warning signs and patterns
  9.2   • You are frightened to ask for transport, food, medical care, or access to your own earnings.
  9.2   • Someone repeatedly takes your wages or mobile-money transfers, or forces you to reveal PINs.
  9.2   • Work, training, education, or business activities are blocked through threats or harassment.
  9.2   • You are pressured to sign loans, sell property, or transfer assets without an informed choice.
  9.2   • A person closely monitors transactions as part of a wider pattern of intimidation.
  9.2   • Money or support is withheld to punish you for seeing friends, seeking care, or refusing demands.
  9.3   These signs invite careful reflection rather than automatic diagnosis of a relationship. A single
  9.3   checklist
  9.3   cannot determine a person's risk. A trained GBV professional can help explore what is happening in
  9.3   context.
 14.5 B 6. Causes, power dynamics, and misconceptions
  9.3   Economic abuse is linked to unequal power, controlling attitudes, gender norms, and opportunities
  9.3   for
  9.3   exploitation. Poverty or unemployment may increase practical vulnerability, but they do not excuse
  9.3   abuse. A person who experiences financial dependence is not responsible for another person's
  9.3   controlling behavior. [4]
  9.3   Myth: Only wealthy people can experience financial abuse. Fact: Control can involve any amount of
  9.3   money or resources.
  9.3   Myth: If one partner earns the money, they are entitled to control all decisions. Fact: Income
  9.3   differences
  9.3   do not justify coercion or denial of dignity.
  9.3   Myth: Economic abuse ends once people separate. Fact: Property disputes, debt, employment
  9.3   disruption, and digital access may continue to affect survivors.
 14.5 B 7. Effects on wellbeing and opportunities
  9.3   Possible effects include chronic stress, fear, loss of confidence, sleep problems, limited mobility,
  9.3   disrupted healthcare, interrupted schooling, job loss, food insecurity, housing instability, and
  9.3   difficulty
  9.3   rebuilding savings. The effects vary between individuals, and some people maintain important
  9.3   sources
  9.3   of strength and support despite abuse. [2, 3, 4]
  9.3   Children may be affected when economic coercion reduces access to basic needs or creates unsafe

  9.3   family conditions. Responding appropriately can require coordinated attention to social support,
  9.3   healthcare, education, and legal safeguards.
 14.5 B 8. Survivor-centred first response
  9.3   A supportive person should listen without judgment, acknowledge what has been shared, ask
  9.3   about
  9.3   immediate needs without demanding details, and offer choices rather than directives. Do not tell
  9.3   the
  9.3   person to confront an abuser, leave immediately, take money, expose records publicly, or disclose
  9.3   passwords. Such actions can increase danger in some situations.
  9.3   Ask whether the conversation, device, and location are safe. Explain privacy and
  9.3   mandatory-reporting
  9.3   limits accurately. If specialized services are available, offer to help the person contact them in a
  9.3   way
  9.3   they choose. WHO's survivor-centred care principles prioritize listening, inquiry about needs,
  9.3   validation,
  9.3   safety, and support. [4, 5]
 14.5 B 9. Financial safety: choice-based considerations
  9.3   Financial safety planning should be individualized with a qualified GBV professional where possible.
  9.3   A
  9.3   person may consider the safety of identity documents, account access, emergency transportation,
  9.3   essential medication, business documents, and trusted contacts. The safest choices depend on
  9.3   monitoring, threats, dependents, and available resources. [3]
  9.2   • Before using online banking or mobile money, consider whether a device, SIM card, account, or
  9.3   notification history is being monitored.
  9.2   • Seek independent information about contracts, loans, property rights, or debts; do not sign under
  9.3   pressure.
  9.2   • If keeping copies of documents would create danger, ask a support worker about safer
  9.2   alternatives.
  9.2   • Discuss potential consequences before changing PINs, accounts, or communications when
  9.2   another
  9.3   person may react violently.
  9.2   • You do not have to report, separate, or disclose every detail to receive respectful information and
  9.3   support, although some services have reporting obligations.

 14.5 B 10. Rwanda-relevant fictional scenarios
 10.6 B Case A - Mobile-money control in a household
  9.3   A woman who sells produce at a market receives payments through mobile money. Her spouse
  9.3   demands her PIN and takes the full balance after each market day. When she asks to keep
  9.3   transport
  9.3   and healthcare money, he threatens to stop her business.
  9.3   Learning point: Taking earnings through threats and preventing access to essential funds may be
  9.3   economic abuse. A supporter should ask about safety and options, not instruct her to change her
  9.3   PIN on
  9.3   a monitored phone.
 10.6 B Case B - Employment interference in Kigali
  9.3   An employee is repeatedly called and threatened by a partner during working hours. The partner
  9.3   appears at her workplace, accuses her of wrongdoing, and says she must resign.
  9.3   Learning point: Interfering with work to increase dependence is employment sabotage. Any
  9.3   workplace
  9.3   response should consider privacy, consent, and the employee's safety.
 10.6 B Case C - Pressured loan in a small business
  9.3   A family member pressures a young adult to sign a loan as guarantor for a business, refusing to let
  9.3   them read the terms and threatening exclusion from the household if they refuse.
  9.3   Learning point: Financial coercion can occur outside intimate relationships. Informed decisions
  9.3   require
  9.3   time, accurate information, and freedom from threats.
 10.6 B Case D - Control of household assets in a rural district
  9.3   A partner sells jointly raised livestock without discussion and refuses to share any proceeds. When
  9.3   questioned, the partner uses threats and says the other person has no right to know about
  9.3   household
  9.3   property.

 10.6 B Case E - Student and education restriction
  9.3   A university student is told by a financially supporting partner that tuition will be withheld unless

  9.3   student ends friendships and abandons a training opportunity.
  9.3   Learning point: Using financial support as leverage to restrict autonomy may indicate coercive
  9.3   control.
  9.3   Support should preserve the student's choice and connect them to appropriate education or social
  9.3   resources.
 14.5 B 11. Five guided learning activities
 10.6 B Activity 1 - Recognize the behavior
  9.3   Read Case A. Identify the conduct that involves controlling funds, the potential effect on
  9.3   independent
  9.3   decision-making, and one safe question a supporter could ask. Suggested response: Threats and
  9.3   taking
  9.3   earnings are concerning; a safe question could be, "Is it safe for us to discuss what support you
  9.3   would
  9.3   find useful?"
 10.6 B Activity 2 - Healthy or coercive?
  9.3   Compare: (a) two partners agree on a savings plan and both can review transactions; (b) one
  9.3   partner
  9.3   uses threats to demand all earnings. Suggested response: The first is potentially healthy
  9.3   cooperation;
  9.3   the second indicates coercive financial control.
 10.6 B Activity 3 - Identify choices without giving orders
  9.3   In Case C, list options for obtaining independent information without telling the person what to do.
  9.3   Suggested response: Explain that a qualified adviser, legal-aid service, or trusted support person
  9.3   may
  9.3   help review obligations. Safety and freedom to choose come first.
 10.6 B Activity 4 - Digital safety reflection
  9.3   A person reports that someone else reads their mobile-money notifications. What should you avoid

 14.5 B 12. Knowledge assessment - questions and explained
 14.5 B answers
 14.5 B 1. Is economic abuse limited to stealing money?
  9.3   Answer: No. It can also include employment sabotage, forced debt, property control, and denying
  9.3   access to essential resources.
 14.5 B 2. Does sharing a household bank account automatically
 14.5 B mean abuse?
  9.3   Answer: No. The key issue is whether control, threats, coercion, or exploitation remove meaningful
  9.3   choice.
 14.5 B 3. Can financial control occur in a family or caregiving
 14.5 B relationship?
  9.3   Answer: Yes. Economic abuse is not limited to intimate partners.
 14.5 B 4. Why might a person remain financially dependent on
 14.5 B someone harmful?
  9.3   Answer: Safety, housing, food, childcare, debt, employment barriers, and other practical conditions
  9.3   may
  9.3   limit options.
 14.5 B 5. Is poverty an excuse for economic abuse?
  9.3   Answer: No. Economic hardship may be a contextual factor, but responsibility belongs to the
  9.3   person
  9.3   using coercion.

 14.5 B 6. Can forced access to mobile-money PINs be
 14.5 B concerning?
  9.3   Answer: Yes, especially when demanded with threats, monitoring, or unauthorized use.
 14.5 B 7. Should a supporter always tell someone to open a
 14.5 B secret account?
  9.3   Answer: No. Financial and digital actions should follow an individualized safety assessment.
 14.5 B 8. Should you demand transaction screenshots to
 14.5 B believe someone?
  9.3   Answer: No. Listen respectfully and avoid unnecessary collection of sensitive personal data.
 14.5 B 9. Can economic abuse continue after separation?
  9.3   Answer: Yes. Debt, property, harassment, and account access may create continuing problems.
 14.5 B 10. What is an appropriate first response?
  9.3   Answer: Listen, validate, ask about immediate safety and needs, explain options and privacy limits,
  9.3   and
  9.3   offer specialist referrals.
 14.5 B 13. Facilitator notes and safeguarding
  9.3   For group education, avoid asking participants to disclose personal experiences. Use fictional
  9.3   cases,
  9.3   allow people to pass, do not collect names or financial information during exercises, and provide
  9.3   private
  9.3   referral options. Do not promise that group discussions are confidential. Facilitators should know
  9.3   how to
  9.3   respond if someone reports immediate danger or a child-safeguarding concern.
  9.3   Rwanda-specific terminology, property and family-law statements, workplace rights,
  9.3   financial-service
  9.3   procedures, and referral contacts must be checked by local GBV/legal professionals before
  9.3   publication.
  9.3   Any digital version should minimize data collection and provide a safe exit option.

 14.5 B 15. Key takeaways
  9.2   • Economic abuse involves coercive control or exploitation of resources and opportunities.
  9.2   • Financial abuse is one part of the broader concept of economic abuse.
  9.2   • Warning signs include withheld necessities, confiscated earnings, forced debt, and work
  9.3   interference.
  9.2   • Financial dependence is never a reason to blame a survivor.
  9.2   • Support should protect autonomy, privacy, and safety, with specialist referral when appropriate.
 14.5 B 16. Glossary
  9.3   Economic abuse: Using resources or economic opportunities to control, exploit, or harm a person.
  9.3   Financial abuse: Misuse or control of money, accounts, property, and financial decisions.
  9.3   Employment sabotage: Interfering with a person's opportunity to obtain or maintain work.
  9.3   Economic exploitation: Taking or using another person's financial resources or labor through
  9.3   coercion or
  9.3   misuse of power.
  9.3   Coercive control: A pattern of conduct that restricts another person's independence through
  9.3   intimidation, threats, or other controlling tactics.
  9.3   Survivor-centred support: Support that prioritizes the person's dignity, safety, informed choice, and
  9.3   needs.
  9.3   Digital financial safety: Considering risks from monitored devices, payment accounts, messages,
  9.3   and
  9.3   transaction information.
