 16.0 B 1. What is sleep, and why do we need it?
  9.4   Sleep is a natural, recurring state in which the brain and body undergo coordinated changes. It is not
  9.4   simply a period of inactivity. During sleep, the brain cycles through different stages that support
  9.4   attention, learning, memory, emotional processing and physical functioning. The value of sleep is not
  9.4   measured by duration alone: timing, continuity and how rested a person feels during the day also
  9.4   matter. [1-3]
  9.4   A single difficult night is common and does not necessarily indicate a disorder. Sleep becomes a
  9.4   greater concern when problems persist, are distressing, or interfere with safe and effective daily life. A
  9.4   person with adequate time in bed may still get poor-quality sleep because of repeated awakenings or
  9.4   an underlying disorder.
 11.3 B Why the topic belongs in mental health education
  9.4   Sleep and mental health affect each other. Stress, anxiety, depression, trauma-related symptoms and
  9.4   other difficulties may interfere with sleep. Repeated poor sleep can also worsen concentration,
  9.4   irritability, emotional regulation and coping capacity. This does not mean that every person who sleeps
  9.4   poorly has a mental illness. [3,4]
 16.0 B 2. The body clock: circadian rhythm
  9.4   The circadian rhythm is an approximately 24-hour timing system that influences when we feel alert,
  9.4   sleepy, hungry and ready for activity. Light, daily activity and regular waking patterns help synchronize
  9.4   it. The brain receives information about environmental light and uses it to coordinate the sleep-wake
  9.4   cycle. The hormone melatonin typically rises as darkness approaches, helping signal nighttime. [2]
  9.4   For example, a student who stays awake on a phone until very late and sleeps until noon on weekends
  9.4   may find it difficult to become sleepy at an earlier school-night bedtime. However, teenagers naturally
  9.4   tend toward later sleep timing than younger children; this biological shift should not be mistaken for

  9.4   laziness.
 11.3 B Sleep pressure
  9.4   A second process is the drive to sleep that builds while we are awake. Sleep pressure generally
  9.4   increases as waking hours pass and decreases during sleep. Together, sleep pressure and the
  9.4   circadian rhythm influence when we fall asleep and wake up. Caffeine can temporarily reduce our
  9.4   sense of sleepiness but does not replace the restorative functions of sleep. [2]
 16.0 B 3. What happens during the night?
  9.4   Typical sleep contains both non-rapid-eye-movement (NREM) and rapid-eye-movement (REM) stages,
  9.4   cycling several times. Patterns change with age and across the night. [5]
  8.2   Stage Broad description
  8.2   NREM 1 A light transition from wakefulness into sleep; easy to awaken.
  8.2   NREM 2 More established sleep; body functions slow and external awareness decreases.
  8.2   NREM 3 Deep or slow-wave sleep; especially prominent earlier in the night.
  8.2   REM Brain activity changes; vivid dreams are common and memory/emotional functions are
  8.2   supported.
  9.4   No single stage is the only "important" stage. Normal sleep includes a balance across stages, and
  9.4   consumer sleep trackers cannot reliably diagnose a sleep disorder based solely on their stage
  9.4   estimates.
 16.0 B 4. How much sleep is generally recommended?
  9.4   Recommended sleep amounts depend on age, and they describe population guidance rather than a
  9.4   rigid score for each individual. Some people need more sleep when ill or recovering from lost sleep.
  9.4   [1,6]

  8.2   6-12 years 9-12 hours per 24 hours

  8.2   61-64 years 7-9 hours
  8.2   65 years and over 7-8 hours

 16.0 B 5. What is insufficient or poor-quality sleep?
  9.4   Insufficient sleep means obtaining less sleep than the body needs. Sleep deficiency can also involve
  9.4   poor timing, interrupted sleep, or a condition that reduces restorative sleep. Possible daytime
  9.4   consequences include low alertness, difficulty remembering information, slower reaction time, changes
  9.4   in mood and greater risk during driving or hazardous tasks. [3]
 11.3 B Common signs worth noticing
  9.2   • Frequently feeling sleepy or unintentionally dozing during the day.
  9.2   • Struggling to concentrate at school, work or during conversations.
  9.2   • Needing much more time than usual to complete simple tasks.
  9.2   • Becoming more irritable or emotionally reactive after poor nights.
  9.2   • Feeling unrefreshed despite giving yourself adequate opportunity to sleep.
  9.4   Symptoms can also arise from anemia, medication side effects, other illnesses, depression or other
  9.4   conditions. A professional assessment can help determine the cause.
 16.0 B 6. Insomnia: more than one bad night
  9.4   Insomnia involves difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep or returning to sleep after waking, despite an
  9.4   adequate opportunity to sleep, with associated distress or daytime impairment. Temporary insomnia
  9.4   may follow stress, illness, travel, grief or major changes. Chronic insomnia is a more persistent clinical
  9.4   pattern and should not be diagnosed from a simple website checklist. [7-9]
 11.3 B Common maintaining patterns
  9.4   A person who worries about whether they will sleep may become increasingly alert at bedtime.
  9.4   Spending extra hours awake in bed, monitoring the clock, or irregular sleep timing can sometimes
  9.4   maintain the problem. Persistent insomnia may also coexist with pain, anxiety, depression, substance
  9.4   use or other sleep disorders.
  9.5   Example: A university student spends several nights awake before examinations. After the
  9.5   examination ends, the student keeps worrying about sleep and begins staying in bed much
  9.5   longer to "catch up." The difficulty continues. A clinician can assess whether insomnia-focused
  9.5   treatment is appropriate.
 16.0 B 7. Other sleep conditions people should know about
 11.3 B Obstructive sleep apnea
  9.4   Sleep apnea may involve repeated breathing interruptions, loud snoring, gasping or choking during
  9.4   sleep, and daytime sleepiness. Not everyone with sleep apnea snores. People with these symptoms
  9.4   should seek medical assessment; sleep hygiene alone cannot treat the underlying breathing disorder.

 11.3 B Circadian rhythm sleep-wake difficulties
  9.4   Some people cannot align their natural sleep timing with work, school or social schedules. Shift work,
  9.4   jet lag and delayed sleep timing may contribute. Treatment depends on the pattern and may require
  9.4   specialist guidance.
 11.3 B Restless legs, parasomnias and hypersomnolence
  9.4   Uncomfortable sensations that improve with moving the legs, unusual behaviors during sleep, or
  9.4   severe daytime sleepiness deserve evaluation. Falling asleep unexpectedly or experiencing sleep
  9.4   attacks should not be assumed to result only from a busy schedule.
 16.0 B 8. How sleep and mental health interact
 11.3 B Stress and worry
  9.4   When the body is on alert, it can be harder to settle at night. Sleep loss can make daily stressors feel
  9.4   harder to manage. Gentle routines may help, but people facing ongoing insecurity or hardship also
  9.4   need practical support.
 11.3 B Anxiety
  9.4   Worry about future events or sleep itself may prolong wakefulness. Persistent sleep difficulties and
  9.4   anxiety symptoms should be assessed together rather than treated as unrelated issues.
 11.3 B Depression
  9.4   Depression may be associated with difficulty sleeping, early waking or sleeping much longer than
  9.4   usual. Changes in sleep alone do not establish depression, and treatment decisions require careful
  9.4   assessment.
 11.3 B Trauma-related symptoms
  9.4   Nightmares, hypervigilance or a feeling of unsafety may disrupt rest. Survivors should not be
  9.4   pressured to close their eyes, disclose memories, or perform relaxation exercises that increase
  9.4   distress.
 11.3 B Bipolar disorder
  9.4   A markedly reduced need for sleep accompanied by unusually high energy, elevated or irritable mood,
  9.4   racing thoughts or impulsive behavior can be a warning sign that needs prompt professional
  9.4   assessment. This is different from ordinary insomnia accompanied by fatigue.
 16.0 B 9. Everyday habits that can support sleep
  9.4   Healthy sleep routines are useful for general wellbeing and can complement clinical care. They are not,
  9.4   on their own, an adequate treatment for chronic insomnia disorder. [1,8-10]
  9.2   • Choose a wake-up time that is as consistent as your responsibilities allow.
  9.2   • Seek natural daylight and appropriate activity during the day.
  7.5   VUGA NDAKUMVA | Mental Health Content Library | Educational draft 5
PAGE 6
  9.2   • Build a predictable winding-down routine: reading, quiet conversation, gentle music or another
  9.2   calming activity.
  9.2   • Reduce bright screen use close to bedtime if feasible, especially when it keeps you alert.
  9.2   • Limit caffeine in the afternoon and evening if it affects you.
  9.2   • Keep the sleep space as dark, quiet, comfortable and safe as reasonably possible.
  9.2   • Avoid using alcohol as a sleep aid: it can fragment sleep and has other risks.
  9.2   • If an environment is unsafe or crowded, focus on feasible improvements and seek practical support
  9.2   rather than blaming yourself.
 16.0 B 10. Practical barriers in Rwanda-relevant daily life
  9.4   A reader may share one room with family members, work evening shifts, study under difficult
  9.4   conditions, manage caregiving duties or have limited privacy. Advice should prioritize realistic actions.
  9.4   For example, a household may coordinate a quieter period, reduce unnecessary night lighting, or plan
  9.4   a more reliable morning routine when possible. These are illustrative situations, not claims about all
  9.4   Rwandan households.
  9.4   When poverty, housing insecurity, severe stress, violence or illness disrupt sleep, an individual routine
  9.4   cannot resolve the underlying problem. Health education should connect people to medical, social and
  9.4   practical support where appropriate.
 16.0 B 11. Evidence-based care for persistent insomnia
 11.3 B What is CBT-I?
  9.4   Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia (CBT-I) is a structured treatment that addresses
  9.4   sleep-related patterns of thinking and behavior. It can include education, tailored behavioral
  9.4   strategies, cognitive approaches and follow-up. Multiple clinical guidelines recommend
  9.4   multicomponent CBT-I as first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults. [8,9]
  9.4   Specialist CBT-I may include methods such as sleep restriction or sleep compression. These should not
  9.4   be copied from a handout without assessment, especially for people with certain medical or psychiatric
  9.4   conditions, excessive daytime sleepiness, high-risk work, or complex sleep problems.
 11.3 B Medication and supplements
  9.4   A clinician may consider medication depending on symptoms, age, other conditions, pregnancy status,
  9.4   interactions and treatment history. Sleeping pills can have side effects and may not address the
  9.4   underlying cause. Do not start, combine, stop or change prescribed medicines on the basis of this
  9.4   chapter. Melatonin is not a universal cure for insomnia and suitability varies. [7,10]

 16.0 B 12. When should someone seek professional help?
  9.4   Consider an appointment with a qualified healthcare professional when sleep difficulty persists,
  9.4   significantly affects mood, concentration, study or work, or occurs alongside loud snoring, breathing
  9.4   interruptions, repeated nightmares, unusual movements, severe daytime sleepiness, chronic pain or
  9.4   symptoms of a mental health condition.
  9.4   Urgent medical assessment is warranted if a person has severe or unusual breathing problems,
  9.4   episodes of losing consciousness, serious injury associated with sleepiness, or new concerning
  9.4   symptoms. If a person is dangerously sleepy, they should not drive or operate machinery. If there are
  9.4   thoughts of suicide or immediate danger, seek urgent emergency assistance through available local
  9.4   emergency services or the nearest emergency department.
  9.4   Where symptoms suggest mania or a significantly reduced need for sleep with marked behavioral
  9.4   changes, seek prompt clinical assessment.
 16.0 B 13. Four everyday learning scenarios
 11.3 B Case 1 - Examination week
  9.4   A secondary-school learner studies very late, uses a bright phone in bed and struggles to wake for
  9.4   class. The learner feels irritable and forgetful.
  9.4   Reflection: What realistic changes to study timing and evening routine might help? How might teachers
  9.4   or caregivers support the learner?
 11.3 B Case 2 - Shift work
  9.4   A healthcare worker rotates between late and early shifts and becomes sleepy during travel. Family
  9.4   responsibilities limit recovery time.
  9.4   Reflection: Which barriers require schedule or workplace support? Why is drowsy travel a safety
  9.4   concern?
 11.3 B Case 3 - Persistent worry
  9.4   An adult worries about falling asleep and checks the clock throughout the night. Sleep problems
  9.4   continue for weeks and affect work.
  9.4   Reflection: What makes this different from a single sleepless night? Why might CBT-I assessment help?
 11.3 B Case 4 - Nighttime breathing concerns
  9.4   A family notices that one adult snores loudly, sometimes gasps during sleep and remains very tired
  9.4   during the day.
  9.4   Reflection: Why should this person seek medical assessment rather than only trying herbal tea or an
  9.4   earlier bedtime?

  9.4   After the week, consider whether patterns change with shift work, phone use, stress, noise, illness,
  9.4   caffeine or caregiving. Bring the notes to a clinician if a sleep problem is ongoing. If recording sleep
  9.4   increases anxiety, stop tracking.
 16.0 B 15. Guided activity B - A gentle evening routine
  9.4   Choose one or two realistic steps, not a rigid checklist: (1) identify tomorrow's priority tasks earlier in
  9.4   the evening, (2) choose a quiet activity for the final part of the day, (3) adjust brightness or noise
  9.4   where feasible, and (4) allow yourself to rest without checking the clock repeatedly.
  9.4   Reflection: Which step is feasible in your home? What barrier would require someone else's
  9.4   cooperation? A routine should be adapted to safety, disability, caregiving and work schedules.
 16.0 B 16. Guided activity C - Distinguishing a worry from a
 16.0 B plan
  9.4   Write down an ordinary concern earlier in the evening if doing so feels comfortable. Divide it into
  9.4   "something I can act on tomorrow" and "something I cannot solve tonight." Choose one practical next
  9.4   action for tomorrow. Do not use this exercise to dismiss real danger, pain or urgent needs.
  9.5   Example: "I may be late for work" can become "I will prepare my bag and check my transport
  9.5   plan." If a concern involves an unsafe environment or a serious medical symptom, practical
  9.5   safety assistance takes priority over relaxation.

 16.0 B 19. Glossary
  9.4   Circadian rhythm: An approximately 24-hour cycle regulating alertness and many bodily functions.
  9.4   Sleep pressure: The biological tendency to become sleepier with time awake.
  9.4   REM: A sleep phase associated with characteristic eye movements and often vivid dreams.
  9.4   NREM: Non-rapid-eye-movement sleep, including lighter and deeper stages.

  9.4   Sleep apnea: A sleep-related breathing disorder that requires medical assessment.
  9.4   Sleep hygiene: Daily routines and environmental practices that can support healthy sleep.
